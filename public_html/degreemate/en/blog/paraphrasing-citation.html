Paraphrasing and citation: practice that takes the worry out of plagiarism

November 11, 2026 / DegreeMate

Plagiarism happens less often through deliberate copying than through reading a source and writing down something too close to it. Even with a citation, keeping the original sentence structure can be a problem. That's why paraphrasing and citing are best practiced together.

How to paraphrase

  1. Read the original until you understand the main point.
  2. Cover it, then write what you remember in your own words.
  3. Compare. If you've only swapped a few words, rewrite the sentence structure.
  4. Keep terms that can't be changed, such as technical terms, and put them in quotation marks.
  5. Cite the source. The idea still belongs to the author, even in your words.

An example

The original sentence and source below are made up for illustration.

OriginalMany first-year students find that managing their time is harder than the coursework itself.

Words swappedLots of first-year students discover that handling their time is more difficult than the classwork itself.

RewrittenFor many students in their first year, the hardest part of college is not the classes but organizing their own schedule (Kim, 2024).

The second sentence changes words but keeps the original structure. The third says the same thing in a new sentence and cites the source.

In-text citation basics

  • APA 7th edition: author's last name and year, for example (Kim, 2024). For a direct quote, add the page: (Kim, 2024, p. 15).
  • MLA 9th edition: author's last name and page, for example (Kim 15). No year.
  • Use whichever style your assignment brief or syllabus asks for.

Common mistakes

  • A source in the reference list with no in-text citation
  • Using one source several times in a paragraph without making clear where its ideas end and yours begin
  • Translating a Korean source into English without citing it. Translated material still needs a citation.
